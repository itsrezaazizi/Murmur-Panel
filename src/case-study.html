Case study · Concept project

From “price?” to a live auto‑reply in under five minutes

Murmur is a design concept for Instagram comment and DM automation. I designed the marketing page and the whole onboarding flow, from sign-up to the first live auto-reply, as one clickable, responsive prototype.

Role
Product design, UX writing, prototyping
Timeframe
2 days
Scope
Landing page + 4-step onboarding
Built with
HTML, CSS, vanilla JS. No framework

✦Honest framing: Murmur isn’t a real product, and nothing here was tested with real users or real traffic. There are no results to report, so you won’t find invented numbers below. What you will find is the reasoning behind each decision and how I would measure it.

01The problem

Small shops answer the same question all day

On Instagram, a post that works brings dozens of identical comments: “price?”, “link?”, “sizes?”. Every hour without an answer is a customer who buys somewhere else.

Comment-to-DM automation already exists. The problem is the setup. Most tools give you a canvas of triggers, conditions and nodes. That works for a marketer. A shop owner replying at midnight between packing orders gives up long before they see any value.

So the brief I set myself was narrow: get a non-technical seller from the landing page to a working auto-reply in under five minutes, without hiding any of the product’s depth.

02Success criteria

What I’d measure

These are the targets the design was built around. They are not results.

North star
< 5 min

Median time from sign-up to the first live auto-reply. The done screen shows this time to the user, so it doubles as an instrumented event.

Activation
1st session

Share of new accounts that switch on at least one auto-reply in the session they signed up in.

Funnel

Landing → sign-up, sign-up → connected account, connected → first reply live. Each step of the wizard maps to one step of the funnel, so drop-off is easy to locate.

Guardrail

Upgrade prompts should not reduce activation. The free path never hits a dead end, and every locked feature has a “Not now”.

03Persona

Designed for Maya, not for a marketer

A proto-persona: built from assumptions about solo sellers, to be checked in interviews. Every screen and every line of copy was checked against her.

M

Maya, owner of Linen Studio

Sells linen clothing on Instagram · 12.4K followers · runs the shop alone, mostly from her phone

Goals

  • Answer every price question fast, even at night
  • Turn comments into orders without living in her DMs
  • Look professional to new customers

Fears

  • Getting her account restricted or banned
  • Handing her password to a third party
  • Paying for something she can’t set up

Context

  • Phone first, short bursts of attention
  • Not technical; “trigger” and “OAuth” mean nothing
  • Already has a post that gets the same comment 50 times
“I don’t want to learn a tool. I want the ‘price?’ comments to stop piling up.”The sentence the landing page headline is written for.
Because she’s on her phone

Mobile-first layouts, a sticky “Start free” bar, a full-screen preview sheet, and controls never under 36px.

Because she’s afraid of bans

Safety is answered where the fear appears: the hero, the connect screen and the first FAQ item.

Because she isn’t technical

No jargon in the interface. Every system term is translated into what it does for her (see section 07).

04Principles

Four rules I used to settle every argument

Value before setup

Show a working result first and let people edit it. A pre-filled example beats an empty form with good labels.

Show, don’t configure

Every change appears on a phone preview instantly. People shouldn’t have to imagine what the customer sees.

Answer the fear where it starts

Trust copy lives next to the action that triggers the doubt, not on a separate security page.

Everything visible, nothing required

All features are discoverable in the builder, but only a keyword and one reply are needed to go live.

05Key decisions

What I chose, and the problem behind each choice

Each decision is tied to a specific problem. If I couldn’t name the problem, the element didn’t make it in.

Landing page

01

The sign-up field lives in the hero

ProblemEvery extra page between interest and sign-up loses people, and the hero is where intent is highest.
DecisionAn email field and “Start free” in the hero. Submitting it skips straight to the code step, and a sticky bar keeps the CTA in reach on mobile.
02

A headline about her moment, and a demo that plays it out

Problem“Automate your Instagram” describes a category. It doesn’t describe the moment Maya feels the pain.
Decision“Answer every ‘price?’ comment in seconds, even while you sleep.” Next to it, a phone loops through the real sequence: a comment, a typing reply, then a DM with a button.
03

Three steps, each with its own time

Problem“Easy setup” is a claim. People need to judge the effort for themselves before they commit.
DecisionA three-step strip with a time on each step (under 1 min, about 1 min, 2–3 min) and a tiny UI of that step, so the promise can be checked against the product later.
04

Twenty features, grouped into four jobs

ProblemA flat grid of 20 feature tiles is unreadable, but hiding features makes paid plans look thin.
DecisionFour bento cards (reply, sell in DMs, capture and follow up, safety and team), each with a small live-looking visual and its list. Plan-gated items carry a plain tag.
05

An FAQ that answers fears, and no fake social proof

ProblemInvented logos, testimonials and stats are easy to spot and cost more trust than they buy.
DecisionThe FAQ opens on the password question and goes straight to bans, account type, limits and setup time. Trust comes from specifics, not from made-up customers.

Onboarding flow

06

A four-step wizard that never moves

ProblemOnboarding that jumps between layouts makes people lose their place, and there was no way back.
DecisionOne 560px frame with labeled steps (Sign up, Connect, Build, Go live), a Back button on every step, and the actions outside the card at a fixed 1/3 to 2/3 split.
07

A code field that gets out of the way

ProblemSix separate boxes are fiddly on a phone, especially when the code arrives by copy and paste.
DecisionFocus moves forward on each digit and back on delete, pasting fills all six, and a full code submits itself. No extra tap needed.
08

Connect: one button, three sentences of trust

Problem“Log in with Instagram” is exactly where a cautious seller stops, and personal accounts fail silently.
DecisionThree short promises (no password, comments and DMs only, disconnect anytime), the professional-account requirement up front, and a collapsible how-to for personal accounts.
09

The builder starts filled in

ProblemAn empty rule builder asks people to design an automation before they’ve seen one work.
DecisionThe form opens with a realistic example: keywords, three reply variations, a DM from a template and a button. Going live means editing text, not making decisions.
10

A live phone preview that follows your cursor

ProblemSettings and the customer’s view live in different places, so mistakes only show up in production.
DecisionA sticky phone renders the comment thread or the DM as you type, and switches tabs to whatever field you’re editing. On mobile it becomes a full-screen sheet.
11

Plan limits appear when they matter

ProblemLocked features with no explanation feel like a bait and switch, and paywalls at sign-up hurt activation.
DecisionLocked blocks show a small lock and a tooltip. Tapping one opens an inline card with what the upgrade adds, the price, and a “Not now” that keeps the free path open.
12

Platform rules as hints, not settings

ProblemRules like the 24-hour messaging window or one DM per person per post are invisible until something silently doesn’t send.
DecisionThey’re stated in one line exactly where they apply, with no toggle to misconfigure.
13

“It’s live”, then prove it

ProblemA success message doesn’t create confidence. Seeing the thing work does.
DecisionThe final screen shows the time it took, a plain summary of the rule, and a step-by-step test (comment received, reply posted, DM sent) you can simulate or try for real.
06Design system

Calm teal, one warm accent

The category is full of blue-to-purple gradients. Murmur needed to feel calm and trustworthy, like someone handling the inbox for you, and still have a human spark.

Lagoon#0E7C70
Deep#0A5A52
Soft#E4F2EF
Butter#FFC94D
Ink#0F1716
Paper#F6F7F5

Teal carries every action. Butter is rationed: a highlighter mark in the headline, the “Most popular” badge, a notification dot in the logo. When it shows up, it means “look here”.

Display
Bricolage

Headlines. Its quirky, optical-size details keep the page from feeling corporate.

Interface
Geist

Body text, labels and buttons. Neutral and very readable at 12–16px.

Numbers
0:59

Geist Mono for timers, steps and code digits, so the numbers don’t jump around.

07Voice & copy

Say what it does for her

Short sentences, second person, sentence case. Buttons say the outcome (“Turn on auto-reply”), errors say how to fix it (“Try something like name@shop.com”).

System termWhat Maya readsWhy
SpintaxVariationsShe understands “a few versions of the reply”, not syntax.
TriggerWhere should we reply?A question she can answer without learning a concept.
Rule / automationAuto-replyThe thing she wants, named after the result.
OAuth / permissionsOfficial Instagram loginNames the safety she’s looking for, not the protocol.
Rate limitingEach person gets one DM per postStates the behavior and the reason: it never feels like spam.
Messaging windowWithin 24 hours of the customer’s last messageA concrete rule she can plan around.
08Scope & next steps

What I left out, on purpose

  • The dashboard after day one: inbox, analytics, managing many auto-replies and team roles. The brief was the first five minutes.
  • Failure states: declined Instagram permissions, a personal account detected after login, an expired code. They’re the next screens I’d design.
  • Billing: upgrades are simulated in the prototype.

Next, I’d run five moderated sessions with solo sellers using this prototype, time each step against the 5-minute goal, and watch the connect screen closely. That’s where I expect the most hesitation.
